A B S T R A C T   
The Domo de San Pedro Geothermal field, located in the westernmost part of the trans-Mexican volcanic belt, is 
part of a silicic volcanic complex. Geothermal energy is produced since 2015 from 9 wells as deep as 3 km. We 
monitor geothermal operations using a continuous seismic dataset from a temporary network of 20 broadband 
stations from March 2021 to January 2022. The well logs describing the geothermal operations allow us to 
investigate the seismic response of the geothermal field to fluid extraction and re-injection across the geothermal 
wells. We produced a catalog of 217 local earthquakes and derive an optimized 1D velocity model for the 
geothermal field between 1.2 m above the sea level (masl) and 12 km depth. Apparent velocity changes (dv/v) 
are measured using seismic ambient noise interferometry from the coda of noise cross-correlations. Next, we 
identify and locate subsurface velocity anomalies, combining the methods of station collapse sensitivity and 
ambient noise imaging applied to the continuous seismic noise records. We distinguish three periods of seismic 
activity where prominent changes in velocity coincide with the re-injection of fluids and significant variation in 
the production rate of the geothermal wells. We determine the distribution of these velocity transients inside the 
geothermal field and propose that they are associated with variations in reservoir fluid content and effective 
stress changes beneath the volcanic system and are responsible for induced microseismicity. Our study shows 
that implementing dv/v quantification as an additional tool for geothermal seismic monitoring can lead to an 
improved understanding of the behavior of the geothermal system over time and ultimately de-risk geothermal 
operations.   
1. Introduction 
Monitoring geothermal systems in the production stage may provide 
relevant information to understand the dynamics of fluid flow at depth 
which is necessary to control geothermal plant operations safely. 
Seismology-based approaches such as microseismic monitoring and 
time-dependent local earthquake to- mography have been carried on 
recently to monitor and characterize geothermal systems across the 
world e.g., (Cal`o et al., 2011; Cal`o and Dorbath, 2013; Taira et al., 2018; 
Toledo et al., 2020; Ge and Saar, 2022). Recently, innovative techniques 
such as the analysis of network coherence and seismic interferometry 
applied in Europe and Mexico (Plan`es et al., 2020; L¨oer et al., 2020; 
Jiwani-Brown et al., 2022; Granados- Chavarrı́a et al., 2022; Cabrer­
a-P´erez et al., 2023a,b) have revealed the velocity structure of 
geothermal systems. 
Transient seismic velocity changes (dv/v) have been identified using 
all the components of seismic sensors for ambient noise interferometry 
in multiple geological environments such as volcanoes, active tectonic 
regions, and geothermal fields (Hillers et al., 2015; Heckels et al., 2018; 
Yates et al., 2019; Cal`o et al., 2021; Machacca et al., 2023). Ambient 
noise interferometry takes advantage of scattered signals repeatedly 
sampling a given volume of crust. In particular, velocity changes are 
computed by retrieving empirical Green’s Functions (GF) via 
cross-correlation of noise records for a pair of stations and measuring the 
time delays of coda waves with respect to a reference GF. The seismic 
coda is sensitive to changes in the mechanical properties of the media as 
small as 0.01 % (e.g., Snieder et al., 2002; Donaldson et al., 2019; 
Brenguier et al., 2020), therefore, a promising tool for monitoring 
transient variations at depth caused by geothermal operations.

2. Geological setting 
The Domo de San Pedro (DSP) is part of a silicic volcanic complex 
located in the westernmost part of the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt 
(TMVB). The TMVB is a continental magmatic arc associated with the 
subduction of the Rivera and Cocos plates beneath the North American 
plate. The TMVB extends E-W for about 1000 km, from the Pacific coast 
to the Gulf of Mexico (Frey et al., 2004; Prol-Ledesma and Mor´an- 
Zenteno, 2019). The DSP develops within a WNW-ESE striking exten­
sional basin (Fig. 1) associated with Na-alkaline and calc-alkaline 
volcanism acting since the late Miocene (Ferrari et al., 2003). The vol­
canic bodies of this region lay to the north of the Cretaceous-Paleocene 
batholith of the Jalisco Block (Ferrari et al., 2000; 2003; Reyes-Orozco 
et al., 2019). The products of this volcanism are the two calc-alkaline 
stratovolcanoes Ceboruco and Tepetiltic, 16 monogenetic cinder 
cones, and more than 20 andesitic and dacitic lava domes. The dacitic 
complex of San Pedro (1980 masl) is the largest of these domes and 
consists of coalesced domes aligned along a WNW-ESE direction. The 
DSP dated between 27 ± 7 and 55 ± 8 Ka and its estimated sub-aerial 
volume is about 4.5 km3 (Ferrari et al., 2003; Frey et al., 2004). The 
vicinity of the DSP is controlled by the regional Compostela (CP) and 
Pedernales (PD) faults trending NW-SE. At the local scale, multiple fault 
systems have been identified with the same regional trending. However 
some faults oriented N-S and NE-SW may control the fluid flow within 
the volcanic system (Castillo and De-La-Cruz, 1992; Reyes-Orozco et al., 
2019; Rodrı́guez et al., 2019).

geothermal provinces for Mexico based on of thermal, geological, and

recharge zone is suggested to occur in the NW of the dome with a minor 
supply from the W and SW of the DSP (Fig. 1). The water in the system 
has little or no contribution from magmatic origin (Rodrı́guez et al., 
2019). 
3. Methods 
In this section, we describe the characteristics of the seismic network 
deployed in the surroundings of the DSPgf. We introduce the methods 
implemented for earthquake detection and location to produce an 
earthquake catalog alongside the methodology to obtain a minimum 1D 
velocity model for the DSPgf. We later present the methods to calculate 
the transient velocity changes by computing cross-correlation functions 
and obtaining differential times of the coda. Finally, we introduce the 
methodology to map the temporal velocity changes in the DSPgf. 
3.1. Seismic network and dataset 
We deployed 20 broadband seismic stations distributed around the 
Domo de San Pedro geothermal field in Nayarit, Mexico (Fig. 1). The 
temporary network was operating from March 2021 to January 2022. 
All the seismic stations worked with DATA-CUBE3ext 24-bit digitizers 
(Digos) with three channels, configured at a gain of 1 and a sampling 
rate of 100 samples per second. The station distribution was denser to­
wards the dome to detect the signals produced by the operation of the 
geothermal wells. The ten months of continuous seismic data have a 
completeness greater than 80 %, and background noise power spectral 
densities are within the standard range of noise models proposed by 
Peterson (1993) (figures S1 and S2. Supplementary material). 
3.2. Earthquake catalog 
We detected and located more than 300 seismic events semi- 
automatically using the code QuakeMigrate (Winder et al., 2020). This 
algorithm uses the seismic waveforms from the entire network simul­
taneously for event detection and provides locations as probability 
density functions. To refine earthquake locations provided by Quake­
Migrate and remove false detections, we manually re-picked 1333 and 
1166 P and S phases, respectively. Then, we relocated 217 actual events 
using the algorithm HYPOCENTER (Lienert, 1994), included in the 
package SEISAN (Havskov et al., 2010). In the absence of a dedicated 
velocity model for the DSP, we used a version of the 1D model of 
Ceboruco volcano composed of five layers and one half- space with 
velocities ranging from 2.5 to 7.7 km/s and a 1.78 Vp/Vs ratio 
(Nú˜nez-Cornú et al., 2020). We calculate the magnitudes implementing 
the coda-duration method (Mc), suitable for micro-seismic to moderate 
magnitude events (Rodríguez-Pradilla and Eaton, 2019). The 217 events 
range in coda magnitude between −2 and 1.6 Mc. The released seismic 
moment, calculated by analyzing the spectral amplitude of 49 seismic 
events with the highest signal-to-noise ratio, is between 1 × 109 and 3.1 
× 1011 N.m. 
3.3. Minimum 1D velocity model 
Kissling (1988) introduced the concept of a minimum 1D velocity 
model calculated by joint hypocenter- velocity inversion of local 
earthquake travel-time data, implemented in the software VELEST 
(Kissling et al., 1994, 1995) and extensively applied in multiple studies 
(Ojeda and Havskov, 2001; Matrullo et al., 2013; Diehl et al., 2017; 
Antunes et al., 2020). We calculated a minimum 1D velocity model for 
the Domo de San Pedro region ranging from the surface to 12 km depth, 
derived from 65 high-quality events (Fig. 2a,b), also obtaining refined 
earthquake re-locations and station corrections in the process (see Sec­
tion 2 of the supplementary material). From this selection of events, we 
inverted 504 travel times of P waves and 468 travel times of S waves. For 
station corrections, we selected station APX as a reference since it was 
located in the center of the network (Fig. 1) and registered the highest 
number of phases. Eight initial velocity models are used for inversion, 
including the simplified velocity model explained in Section 3.2 and 
other models published for western Mexico and Ceboruco Volcano 
(Nú˜nez-Cornú et al., 2002, 2020; Ochoa-Ch´avez et al., 2015), along with 
several arbitrary velocity models (Fig. 2c.) as suggested in Kissling et al. 
(1995) to reduce inversion biases. The inversion uses a constant Vp/Vs 
ratio of 1.70, which we constrained with a Wadati diagram analysis over 
individual earthquakes considering correlation values greater than 0.9. 
After the inversion, we obtained several output models spanning a 
narrower range of velocities with depth (red-shaded area in Fig. 2d). We 
test about 5000 velocity models re-locating the 65 earthquakes with a 
grid search method. We selected the model with the lowest travel time 
residuals as the velocity model for the DSPgf (Fig. 2d red bold line). 
3.4. Transient velocity changes 
The reconstructed Green Functions (GFs) derived from inter-station 
cross-correlations of ambient noise records can reveal changes in the 
subsurface’s seismic velocities (Lecocq et al., 2014; Obermann et al., 
2015). The cross-correlations are two-sided time functions with positive 
(causal) and negative (acausal) correlation lags representing waves 
traveling in opposite directions between the two stations (Bensen et al., 
2007). The coda of the GFs is composed of scattered waves that samples 
the subsurface repeatedly, which makes it particularly sensitive to small 
changes in velocity (Snieder et al., 2002; Donaldson et al., 2019 ; 
Brenguier et al., 2020). The relative time delay registered in the coda of 
the GFs corresponding to different recording periods is directly related 
to the relative change in subsurface velocity, assuming it is distributed 
homogeneously over the studied volume, as follows:

variations over ten months of continuous seismic records, using the nine 
tensor components (figures S5 to S7 supplementary material), by 
implementing the Moving-Window Cross-Spectrum (MWCS) technique, 
implemented by Lecocq et al. (2014) within the code MSNoise. The 
workflow begins with a pre-processing stage applied to the whole data 
set. Then, we compute the cross-correlation functions (CCFs) and sub­
sequent stacking of the CCFs for different time spans for each station 
pair. Next, we measure the travel time arrivals between the “current” 
CCFs and the reference cross-correlation functions (RCC). Finally, we 
calculate the average travel-times delays over all the station pairs to 
assess the relative velocity changes in the study area (Lecocq et al., 
2014). Additional details on the workflow can be found in the supple­
mentary material (Section 5).

3.5. Mapping of temporal velocity changes in the DSPgf 
To image the spatial distribution of the dv/v and its evolution within 
the system, we identified in the dv/v time series the most prominent 
velocity variations. For each identified period of prominent dv/v fluc­
tuation, we averaged the dv/v of each station pair in the considered time 
period and located the change in space via a tomographic inversion 
approach composed of three steps. First, we implemented a modified 
version of a standard 2D tomography approach (Barmin et al., 2001) as 
described in Cal`o et al. (2021). This method assumes that the coda 
sensitivity is mainly distributed along the trajectory between station 
pairs. Second, we reconstructed the velocity kernel underneath the 
station sites using the “station collapsed sensitivity” method introduced 
by Brenguier et al. (2014). This approach assumes that most of the dv/v 
sensitivity is concentrated beneath the stations (Pacheco and Snieder, 
2005; Obermann et al., 2013). The third step consists in reconstructing 
the final dv/v spatial distribution combining the two imaging techniques 
discussed above using partition coefficients (Cal`o et al., 2021). Ober­
mann et al. (2019) demonstrated that the 3D location of velocity 
changes can be obtained by computing the full sensitivity kernel 
combining the sensitivities of scattered body and surface waves using 
partition coefficients (PC). Body waves dominate the inter-station tra­
jectory and the surface waves dominate the sensitivity beneath the 
stations. Then, we combine the dv/v of steps 1 and 2 using a grid of 
0.55×0.55 km (resolution tests Section 8 of the supplementary material) 
and partition coefficients from 0 to 1 with steps of 0.25. PC of 0 stands 
for images computed using ray-tracing tomography, and PC of 1 corre­
sponds to images retrieved using the station collapsed sensitivity 
method. To map the dv/v transients, we used central frequencies of coda 
waves of the GF’s of 0.5 and 0.6 Hz corresponding to GF’s filtered at 
frequencies of 0.4–0.8 and 0.1–0.9, a time lag window width of 30 s, and 
inter-station distances shorter than 2 km. We finally show the spatial 
dv/v results as two kinds of maps: absolute dv/v values (directly ob­
tained from the time delays) and dv/v deviations from the mean. See 
Section 6 of the supplementary material. 
4. Results 
We introduce the re-location of the seismicity registered at the DSP 
during the ten months of seismic monitoring using a 1D velocity model 
developed for the region. We also show the average production and re- 
injection rates of fluids at the geothermal field. Then, we utilize all the 
described information to contrast against the temporal velocity varia­
tions calculated at the DSPgf during the monitoring campaign. Finally, 
we show the space-time evolution of the most prominent transient ve­
locity perturbations in the geothermal field.

5.1. Earthquakes distribution 
We document for the first time the occurrence of seismic activity in 
the region of the DSP that has events lower than 1.6 Mc. We introduce an 
optimized velocity model that yields locations with 30 % lower RMS 
than earthquake locations with previous regional models. The

distribution along depth of the relocated earthquakes shows two clus­
ters. A shallow cluster mostly confined within the geothermal reservoir 
(i.e., between 0.5 km and 2.5 km depth). We also identify a second group 
of events from 2.5 to 7 km depth. While our observations indicate that 
the shallow cluster is probably associated with geothermal operations 
(see discussion herein), we cannot assess the causative nature of the 
deeper seismicity. Hence, to establish a possible link between velocity 
changes and anthropogenic activity, we consider only the seismicity that 
we relocated into the geothermal reservoir. The three periods of most 
abundant seismicity at the DSPgf coincide with the most intense hy­
draulic activity conducted onsite. This seismicity increases when fluids 
re-injection reaches the maximum (Period 1 Fig. 4a,b) or the re-injection 
suddenly decreases (Period 2 Fig. 4a,b). Furthermore, the seismicity 
displays a swarm-like behavior, with no clear mainshock-aftershocks 
evolution, typical of fluid-driven seismicity (Hainzl, 2004; Chen et al., 
2012; Hainzl et al., 2012; Shelly et al., 2013; Cox, 2016), in contrast to 
periods of relatively stable geothermal operations (i.e., starting from day 
200 onwards). An interesting lack of seismicity was observed between 
days 40 and 50 during simultaneous re-injection/production of about 
the same order of magnitude that may have compensating effects, 
leading to a pore pressure equilibrium in the shallow subsurface. We 
notice that the depth of the shallow seismicity is the same as the bottom 
wells where operations were conducted (Fig. 3b). This corresponds to 
the proposed depth of the reservoir where magnetotelluric surveys 
(Reyes-Orozco et al., 2019) found a highly fractured fluid circulation 
zone between the isotherms of 150 and 250 ◦C (Rodrı́guez et al., 2019). 
In line with previous studies (e.g., Gaucher et al., 2015; Guglielmi et al., 
2015; McGarr, 2014; Lin and Wu, 2018; Ge and Saar, 2022), we propose 
that pore pressure variations may promote microseismic activity in 
geothermal reservoirs. The shallow seismicity spreads out horizontally 
about 4 km from the geothermal wells. Zones at that distance are sug­
gested to be close enough to be affected by the causal effects of hori­
zontal pore pressure variations linked to re-injected fluids. These reduce 
effective stress on fractures in a critical state inducing seismicity (Sha­
piro et al., 1997; Shapiro and Dinske, 2009; Terakawa et al., 2012; Ge 
and Saar, 2022). The deeper seismicity cluster spans from 2.5 km (i.e., 
the bottom-wells of re- injections) to about 7 km (Fig. 3). Rodrı́guez et al. 
(2019) inferred the isotherms of the DSPgf using geothermometers 
showing that the maximum depth of the geothermal wells (3 km from 
the surface) reaches about 300 ◦C. Temperatures obtained with geo­
themometers can show large uncertainties. Instead, stabilized temper­
atures could better constrain the thermal structure (Nitschke et al., 
2017; Ystroem et al., 2020). Nevertheless, we assume that temperatures 
underneath the DSPgf at >7 km depth might be above ≈500 ◦C, over­
coming the brittle-ductile transition zone (Parisio et al., 2019; Cheng 
and Ben-Zion, 2019), which can explain the absence of deeper seis­
micity. Although seismicity in the deep and shallow clusters occurs 
generally at the same time (figure S8 supplementary material), it is 
unclear if the fluids re-injected/extracted within the reservoir may be 
linked to the seismicity in the deeper cluster. The lack of a spatial cor­
relation between the reservoir and the deep cluster makes it challenging 
to speculate about the origin of the deep seismicity. For instance, a 
possible trigger may be related to the perturbation of local fault systems 
by pore pressure diffusion fronts. This has been proposed in multiple 
studies (e.g., Goebel et al., 2017; Porter et al., 2019), including pore 
pressure diffusion in groundwater wells (e.g., Shalev et al., 2023;

may caused by a ductile rheology, possibly related to magma storage. 
The occurrence of such magma may justify the elevated geothermal

Period 3 (Fig. 4, days 150 to 165) when production suddenly declined.

5.2. dv/v changes and spatial distribution 
The unique dataset we have available of the DSPgf allows us to seek 
the association of the seismic velocity changes with geothermal opera­
tions. We investigate station pair cross-correlations in frequency bands 
ranging from 0.1 to 1 Hz using central periods from 1 to 3.5 s. For the 
dv/v calculations, the chosen time window of the cross-correlations 
corresponds to the early coda where scattered Rayleigh waves are sug­
gested to dominate (Obermann et al., 2013) and Love waves can also be 
present. Rayleigh waves dominate seismic signals retrieved on CCfs, 
particularly on components that combine vertical and/or radial chan­
nels (ZZ, ZR, RZ, RR). Therefore, a proportion of Rayleigh energy is 
expected on both N and E channels when looking at unrotated horizontal 
components. By averaging all station pairs, Rayleigh energy is present 
on both N and E channels. Since Rayleigh energy is known to be present 
on the vertical channel, we assume it is present on ZZ, EZ, ZE, NZ, and 
ZN components (figure S9). For consistency, we display a simplified 
combination of vertical and horizontal components to represent the 
dv/v in the wavefield. The method can be affected by the stations array 
geometry, the inter-station distance, and topography. Resolution test 
performed to describe the spatial distribution of dv/v variations at the 
subsurface of the DSPgf at a period of 1 s (figure S6 of the supplementary 
material) demonstrates the resolution of our mapping. This corresponds 
to the shallow geothermal system under the DSPgf (see velocity kernel of 
the DSPgf. Figure S11 of the supplementary material). The dv/v dia­
grams of Fig. 4d, e, f, and the ones available in the supplementary ma­
terial (figures S8 and S9) describe the apparent velocity changes 
sampled by the scattered coda waves (Snieder et al., 2002). The most 
prominent dv/v (P1, P2, and P3) changes at the DSPgf coincide with 
active geothermal operations and occur when such activities change 
significantly in less than 24 h. One exception is a prominent negative 
dv/v change visible at about 130 days of monitoring using a GF filtered 
from 0.4 to 0.8 Hz (Fig. 4e). This negative dv/v change matches the 
occurrence of the greatest earthquake registered (1.8 Mc, Fig. 4a), 
whose epicentre is within the DSPgf but does not coincide with any 
geothermal operation (Fig. 4b,c). Fig. 4e shows the dv/v changes of 
Period 1 when the dv/v drop of 0.15 % coincides with the highest fluids 
re-injection (> 140 m3/h) and with the lowest extraction. Apparent 
velocity changes of similar magnitude have been reported in other 
geothermal fields associated with fluids re-injection (Ojo et al., 2022), 
volcanic unrest (Cubuk-Sabuncu et al., 2021b), and large earthquakes 
(Taira et al., 2018). The dv/v returns gradually to its pre-injection value 
(± 0.05 %) as the re-injection rate of fluids decreases. The maximum 
dv/v drop coincides with the most abundant occurrence of earthquakes. 
This suggests a seismic response of the geothermal system to high rates 
of fluid re-injection and the steady depletion of the production rate. This 
also emerges while analyzing Period 2 where the production rate is 
constant for more than three months, and the only significant change is a 
sudden drop of re-injection of fluids lasting about 10 days. We observe a 
similar prominent increment of dv/v (0.2 %) in Period 2 when the 
re-injection rate drops to 12.5 m3/h around days 93–100 (Fig. 4d) with a 
concurrent increment of seismic activity and a sudden jump in the cu­
mulative seismic moment (Fig. 4a). The system recovers its original state 
(i.e., ± 0.05 %) once the re- injection rates recover their background 
levels. Numerical simulations have shown that changes in the number 
and pattern of fractures and fluid circulation in an elastic media produce 
changes in seismic velocity (Sawayama et al., 2022). We attribute the 
dv/v variations to pore pressure changes in the system induced by the 
abrupt variations in re-injection rates that create or perturb 
critically-stressed fractures and faults promoting simultaneously 
micro-seismicity (Taira et al., 2018; S´anchez-Pastor et al., 2019). Other 
possible mechanisms that cause changes in the elastic properties of the 
media are deformation (e.g., creation of cracks and fractures) and 
changes in pore pressure, temperature and pressure. Pore pressure 
diffusion also may induce decreasing of the friction coefficient between 
clays within pre-existing fracture and cause mechanical weakening of 
the host rock leading to failure (Talwani and Acree, 1984; Ge and Saar 
2022). We expect changes in pore pressure to more likely cause a ve­
locity change significant enough to be observable. Seasonal effects may 
cause periodic long-duration variations in dv/v curves (on the scale of 
months) if there are significant variations in the distribution of 
low-frequency noise sources or significant changes in surface tempera­
ture, and natural hydrological variations (e.g., precipitation, soil mois­
ture content, recharge level of shallow aquifers), which may mainly 
affect the shallower subsurface (e.g., < 100 m). For periods 1 and 2, the 
main velocity changes are successfully located and presented in Figs. 5 
and 6. The time-dependent tomograms allowed us to track the dynamics 
of the geothermal site and observe the most likely location of subsurface 
velocity changes. In both cases (Period 1 and 2), we highlight that the 
dv/v source is near the geothermal wells. The dv/v anomaly resulted of 
Period 1 (Fig. 5) is clearer than the one observed for the Period 2 (Fig. 6). 
This may be due to the longer duration of the dv/v transient for Period 1 
(14 days) compared to Period 2 (6 days). We note that it is unlikely that 
the dv/v method could locate subsurface velocity transients shorter than 
a few days such as the ones that could be induced by sudden 1-day-long 
variations of the re-injection rates (e.g., days 72, 90, and 182 Fig. 4b), 
which are not associated with prominent peaks in the dv/v curves. The 
dv/v transient observed in Period 3 differs from periods 1 and 2. A 
prominent positive peak of 0.26 % in dv/v occurs between days 157 to 
161, matching the ending of a sudden decrease in production rate 
alongside a pressure drop registered on the production wells (Fig. 4c). 
The brine re-injection rate fluctuates around a background value of 80 
m3/h, with no major changes. The spatial distribution of this P3 dv/v 
peak (Fig. 7) suggests the source of the velocity transient was located in 
the surroundings of the DSPgf. Here, we point out that there is no 
concomitant micro-seismicity. Studies found aseismic velocity varia­
tions may still occur as a result of changes in the fluid re- injection in the 
reservoir (Rivet et al., 2016; Taira et al., 2018). 
Our high-resolution seismic catalog, coupled with a detailed 
schedule of the geothermal operations, allows us to derive a conceptual 
model linking geothermal procedures and velocity changes across the 
DSPgf (Fig. 8). Although it is difficult to rule out certain combinations of 
conditions that promote prominent dv/v changes, we argue that sig­
nificant variations of the apparent velocity are observed as a conse­
quence of sudden dynamic changes in fluid circulation and pore pressure 
within the geothermal reservoir. This seems not to be reflected by the 
background pressure registered at the well-heads. Reductions in velocity 
may occur due to fluids saturation into the system (both water and steam 
phases), increasing the overall pressure of the reservoir as discussed by 
De Matteis et al. (2008) based on the modeling of wave velocity as a 
function of porosity and fluids saturation in a geothermal field. Instead, 
the positive dv/v may respond to the depletion of fluids, causing 
porosity to increase and pressure to decrease, leading to an increase in 
seismic wave velocity. We observe that for the DSPgf sudden changes in 
the re-injection of fluids produce changes in seismic velocity and can

trigger microseismicity. Fig. 8a represents a perturbation of the me­

6. Conclusion 
We introduce a seismic catalog and an optimized 1D minimum ve­
locity model for earthquake location in the Domo de San Pedro, Mexico. 
We also analyzed the seismic response of the DSPgf to activities of 
extraction and re-injection of fluids using ambient noise interferometry. 
We demonstrate that prominent velocity changes are related to sudden 
changes in the re-injection of fluids and sharp changes in the extraction 
of geothermal fluids, regardless of the well-heads’ pressure. Under 
certain conditions, such as constant extraction and sudden change of 
fluids re-injection, induced micro-seismicity. Analysis of the spatial 
distribution of the dv/v can reveal the evolution of the stress state of the 
geothermal field in response to the fluid dynamics. Combining classical 
earthquake monitoring methods, geothermal operation logs, and seismic 
ambient noise interferometry constitute a new approach to geothermal 
exploitation monitoring. This workflow can be implemented in real- 
time and provide hints for improving the current geothermal models.
