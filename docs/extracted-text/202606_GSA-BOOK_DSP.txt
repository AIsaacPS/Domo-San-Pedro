ABSTRACT
The San Pedro Dome geothermal field is located on the northeastern limit of the 
Compostela graben, at the western of the Tepic-Zacoalco rift, southwest of the Nayarit 
state in Mexico. Despite detailed geophysical surveys around the San Pedro Dome 
conducted by private companies to locate the permeable hydrothermal reservoir, very 
little is known about the heat source and structures that conform to the geothermal 
system. To investigate the role of the identified geoelectric structures in the region and 
the general distribution of the geothermal reservoir, we inverted the full impedance 
tensor of a set of 11 magnetotelluric soundings in the range of 10

1. INTRODUCTION
The Compostela graben is the westernmost structure that 
conforms to the Tepic-Zacoalco rift, which is the limit between 
the Jalisco Block and Sierra Madre Oriental (Fig. 1). The San 
Pedro Dome (SPD) is located on the northeastern boundary of 
the Compostela graben, which in turn is a consequence of the 
extensional tectonic regime of the Tepic-Zacoalco rift in this 
particular area.
The geophysical prospection in the SPD started 10 years 
ago, initially focusing on identifying suitable locations for pro-
ductive geothermal wells. Following two costly failures primarily 
based on surface geochemical and physical evidence, the strategy 
changed, and 30 audio-magnetotelluric soundings were carried 
out in a specific spot area, constrained by detailed structural and 
geologic studies. The combined results pointed toward the cur-
rent production zone of the SPD (red spots in Fig. 2), where the 
first well drilled was highly productive. Several more productive 
wells have been drilled in this area, contributing to the known 
geothermal energy potential of the country. Nearly 120 additional 
magnetotelluric (MT) soundings were acquired, mainly in the 
N-NW of the SPD to better define the structure of the geothermal 
field. However, these results and data are not public; the current 
installed capacity of the SPD geothermal field in 2020 was of 
35.5 MW (Orozco et al., 2020).
Analysis of drilled well stratigraphy identifies four main 
geological formations, spanning from the late Pliocene and Qua-
ternary to the Cretaceous-Paleogene basement rocks (Orozco et 
al., 2019), each with different degrees of alteration. Neverthe-
less, the depth of the wells (<3 km) is insufficient to shed light

2. SPD TECTONIC SURROUNDINGS
The  Tepic-Zacoalco  rift  forms  a  nearly  400-km-long  
NW-SE–trending tectonic boundary between the Sierra Madre 
Occidental to the north and the Jalisco Block to the south (Fig. 1). 
The NW end of the Tepic-Zacoalco rift met at a right angle with 
the Bahía de Banderas Graben, while the SE end joins the Graben 
de Colima rift at the Guadalajara Triple Point. The time-elapsed 
extensional process triggered at ca. 20 Ma (Rosas-Elguera et al., 
2003) has led to a segmented Tepic-Zacoalco rift consisting of 
separated graben and semi-graben structures containing or neigh-
boring with isolated volcanic fields as recently as the end of the 
last ice age. The Compostela graben, bounded by normal fault is 
a complex extensional NW-SE basin, located at the westernmost 
end of the Tepic-Zacoalco rift, and with the same orientation. 
It has been defined as a Neogene complex extensional basin 
trending WNW-ESE, developed in the northernmost part of the 
Tepic-Zacoalco rift in western Mexico (Ferrari et al., 2003). In 
western Mexico, the production of the volcanism is related to 
the subduction of the Rivera and Cocos plates beneath the North 
American plate along the Middle American Trench, but also to the 
detachment of the Baja Peninsula that started with the Neogene.

The  Compostela  graben  is  bounded  to  the  north  by  the  
 Milpillas–Cerro Grande fault and to the south by the Borbollón 
fault. The SPD is located on top of the northern fault where 
the stratigraphy is known from the drilled wells, encountering: 
dacitic and rhyolitic flows (0.6–0.1 Ma) with thickness above  
700 m; 150 m of andesites and dacites of the Pliocene; andesites, 
basaltic andesites, and basalts of the Miocene; and granodiorite 
and diorite from the late Paleocene and upper Cretaceous, asso-
ciated to the basement of the Jalisco Block, which is bounding 
to the south of the Compostela graben.
The role of faulting in relation to the geothermal reservoir 
is not fully understood in this extensional volcanic context. The 
Milpillas–Cerro Grande fault acts as a retention barrier of the 
SPD geothermal field, but there must be highly permeable verti-
cal paths driving fluids at high pressures. Mapping the fractured 
granitic basal rocks is therefore of key importance in assessing 
the geothermal potential resources of the volcanic region within 
the Compostela graben.
3. THE MT DATA AND 3-D MODELING
The MT method has been successfully employed for geo-
thermal exploration as a main tool in the prospecting phase 
previous to the full development of the resource (Spichak and 
Manzella, 2009; Cumming, 2009; Piña-Varas et al., 2014; Muñoz, 
2014; Arzate et al., 2018, Corbo-Camargo et al., 2020; Guevara 
et al., 2020; Pereyra Quiroga et al., 2023). This work presents 
the results obtained with 11 broadband MT soundings acquired 
mainly within the Compostela graben (Fig. 2). The data were 
collected using Phoenix MTU-5A equipment and proprietary 
software to process the data.
Phase Tensor
Before conducting formal MT data inversion, certain geo-
electric features of the SPD are observed in the MT responses. 
One transfer function that describes the relationship between the 
electric and magnetic fields is the phase tensor (PT; Caldwell 
et al., 2004; Booker, 2014), which is independent of galvanic 
distortions caused by near-surface heterogeneities. The PT is rep-
resented as an ellipse as a function of the period for each station 
in a plane or along a profile (Fig. 3), and the magnitude of the 
principal axes is a function of the maximum and the minimum 
value of the PT, while the skew (angle β) defines the degree of 
symmetry. Therefore, the ellipticity indicates the dimensional-
ity of the structure in the subsurface, whether it is 1-D and/or 
2-D/3-D; if β is ~0, the medium is 1-D or 2-D. Also, a PT value 
>45° suggests a transition to a more conductive medium (hot 
colors in Fig. 3). Values <45° (cold colors in Fig. 3) imply that 
the resistivity increases with depth.
In Figure 3, in the range of frequencies above 10 Hz (left 
panel), the PT exhibits a circular shape (Fig. 3B) and shows 
low β-angles (Fig. 3D), suggesting a 1-D structure. However, at 
some higher frequencies (above 100 Hz) in MT soundings 1–4, 
the β-angles are elevated, likely due to distortive effects from 
inductive phenomena caused by topography. The phases >45° 
indicate a transition to a more conductive medium.
Between 10 Hz and one second, there is a transition from 
conductor to resistor featuring phases below 45°. This transition 
is interpreted as for those frequencies, the induction volume has 
encountered the presence of the granite basement of the Jalisco 
Block complex. The ellipses show high dispersion in terms of 
elongation and orientation but maintain low β-angles, suggesting 
the presence of faults and fractures. Below one second, the β- angle  
increases in all MT soundings, indicating a 3-D medium. At high 
periods greater than 10 s, the phases suggest the presence of a 
more conductive medium, possibly indicating the brittle-ductile 
transition zone.
Inverse Modeling
One-dimensional modeling of the apparent resistivity and 
phase curves as a function of frequency was performed to obtain 
the average conductivity structure of the subsurface, by inverting 
the Berdichevsky invariant of Z. Figure 4 shows a comparison of 
Occam 1-D models (Constable et al., 1987) for MT-1 and MT-2, 
which are located close to the wells SP3 and SP7–8, respectively. 
Both wells reach the upper part of the granite, characterized by 
its high electrical resistivity. Within the upper 1,000 masl, a con-
ductive layer correlates with altered andesites and dacites that 
forms an impermeable seal for pressurized fluids. Toward MT-2, 
the thickness of the seal increases and becomes more conductive  
(~5 Ohm-m). Overlaying the conductive seal lies a layer of unal-
tered resistive rhyolitic lava flows from the later volcanic activity 
of the SPD (Ferrari et al., 2003; Orozco et al., 2019).
The less resistive the granite is at depth, the more fractures 
and fluids it may have. High conductivities in a resistive rock 
matrix are largely related to the highly mineralized water and to 
the permeability distribution of the rock which hosts the hydro-
thermal reservoir. The natural electromagnetic fields on which 
the MT method is based are used to trace pathways along contact 
surfaces or faulted terrain where hot mineralized fluids remain 
trapped during dormant periods beneath the conductive sealing 
clay cap layer. This layer is primarily characterized by smectite 
minerals at temperatures ranging from 70 to 150 °C, and by 
precipitated magnetite and ferromagnesian minerals at higher 
temperatures ranging from 150 to 300 °C (e.g., Jording et al., 
2000; Cumming and Mackie, 2010; Corbo-Camargo et al., 2020; 
Hering et al., 2021; Arzate et al., 2023).
We inverted the full impedance tensor of the MT soundings 
using the 3-D inversion algorithm ModEM (Egbert and Kelbert, 
2012; Kelbert et al., 2014). The model mesh comprised 51 cells 
of 800 m of lateral extension covering the survey area. Vertically, 
the mesh consisted of 42 cells with an increasing thickness by a 
factor of 1.2 from an initial value of 50 m. Padding horizontal 
cells of 15 × 15 increment of a factor of 1.3 were included to 
avoid the boundary effects (Fig. 5A). The mesh included the 
topography to account for inductive distortion (Jiracek, 1990;

ANOMALOUS CONDUCTORS
The  results  are  now  analyzed  as  four  plane  views  (Figs.  
7A–7D).  The  figure  displays  the  distribution  of  resistivity  at  
different elevations. The arrows indicate the direction from the 
shallower (700 masl) to the deepest (350 mbsl). The 700 masl 
section (Fig. 7A) corresponds to a subsurface depth of ~600 m 
within the domain of the SPD. At this elevation, the anomalous 
low resistivity (<10 Ohm-m), correlates to the top of the altered cap 
layer as confirmed through the drilled wells in the SPD geothermal 
field. A similar anomaly, either in size and conductivity values, 
is observed south of the area in the surroundings of the Amado 
Nervo and Borbollón faults. However, neither of these resistivity 
anomalies exhibits a magnetic response (Fig. 7E). This absence of 
magnetic response, especially in the context of the SPD, directly 
confirms that the high electrical conductivity of the mineralized 
cap layer is not influenced by ferromagnesian or magnetite-rich 
minerals, which may be also the case for the southern Amado 
Nervo fault and Borbollón fault high conductivity anomaly.

center of the Compostela graben, more related with the Amado 
Nervo fault and Borbollón fault anomalous conductive zone 
rather than with to the SPD. Except for the SE (Fig. 7B), most 
of the area becomes highly resistive, unveiling compact unaltered 
granitic basement rocks. The outlined high magnetization zone 
only marginally matches the conductor. At mean sea level (Fig.

6. DISCUSSION OF RESULTS
The correlation of the cross sections with the lithology 
reported from drilled wells allows the calibration of the low 
resistivity  layer  (<10  Ohm-m)  with  an  ~500-m-thick  layer  
of argillized Miocene andesitic-dacitic rocks, considered the 
sealing cap of the reservoir. Argillization occurring within a 
temperature range of 50–200 °C (Browne, 1978) produces 
electrically conductive clay layers of smectite and smectite- 
illite. Beneath this highly conductive clay layer, the resistivity 
increases to ~100 Ohm-m even though the temperature continues 
to rise. Temperatures exceeding 220–240 °C lead to propylitic 
alteration, mainly in the form of chlorite and epidote (e.g., 
Ussher et al., 2000), with a reported resistivity ranging from 10 
to 100 Ohm-m (Wright et al., 1985; Pellerin et al., 1996). In the 
SPD geothermal system, the propylitic alteration is associated 
with an anomalous resistivity range of 30–80 Ohm-m, extend-
ing to greater depths in some sectors, and toward the eastern 
sector of the Compostela graben (Figs. 8 and 9). The propylitic

the Compostela graben, suggesting that the source of hot min-

the central and southeast region of the Compostela graben. In 
this context, the fault surface structures, such as the Milpillas–

those inferred from high resistivity gradients from the 3-D model 
(dashed dark lines), seem to act as impermeable barriers rather 
than as permeable pathways; fluids appear to follow a pre-faulted, 
more permeable pathway through the granite while avoiding 
the argillized, impermeable fault system of central Compostela 
graben inner faults.
In particular, given the results and previous arguments, we 
find a close relationship between the deep fault system of the 
SPD and the late Miocene faulting of the granitic basement rocks, 
which is particularly less dense toward the SE of the studied 
area (see Fig. 1). The reduction in density is naturally attributed 
to an increase in pore fluid within a fractured granite matrix, 
which consequently decreases the electrical resistivity. Figures 
9A and 9B illustrate these arguments, where the thick lines on 
top of the mapped surface faults indicate that these outcropping 
structures are impermeable barriers made of argillized minerals. 
Additionally, we identify two distinct potential geothermal res-
ervoirs (GR), separated by a confined zone of high conductivity 
and high magnetization. GR-1 is the SPD geothermal reservoir, 
bounded to the south by the Milpillas–Cerro Grande fault, and 
GR-2 represents a potential geothermal reservoir associated with 
the Amado Nervo volcano. A composite 3-D representation of the 
Compostela Graben geothermal system is presented in a video 
clip (Movie.wmv; see footnote 1) synthesizing the main results 
of this MT survey.
7. CONCLUSIONS
Wide-band magnetotelluric (MT) soundings have become 
the main tool in geothermal prospecting, not only because of 
the deeper exploration depths reached that allows heat source 
modeling, but also due to their outstanding resolution at high 
frequencies (10
3
–10
0
 Hz), offering a detailed near-surface more 
detailed prospecting.
The present study contributed to the understanding of the 
SPD active geothermal field and for the semi-regional geother-
mal system of the Compostela Graben. This geophysical work 
must be considered from the point of view of future monitor-
ing of an active geothermal fields to observe and understand 
possible changes in physical proxies. The integrated model 
provides a useful and robust tomographic imagery of the SPD

The calibrated structural subsurface model, derived from

understanding on the role of some of the main faults confining
